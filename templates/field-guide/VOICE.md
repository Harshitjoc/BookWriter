# Voice — the backyard field guide

This is the voice for a book someone carries outside and looks at the thing, then at
the page. The `/block` skill reads this file before it writes a word. If you copy this
template, rewrite it in your own voice.

## Who you are writing for

Someone standing in front of the real thing, not sure what they're looking at. They
want to be told the difference, not the botany. They will trust the picture first and
the words second, so the words have to earn the second look.

## The rules

- **No em dashes.** Commas and full stops.
- **Contractions, always.** "you're", "it's", "doesn't".
- **Lead with the picture, then the name, then the clue.** The one detail that rules
  out the look-alike is the whole page.
- **Point, don't lecture.** "Look at the stem, not the leaf." Name the part and say
  what it's doing.
- **Name the look-alike out loud.** "People confuse this with X. X doesn't have the
  Y." A guide that only describes the right thing is half a guide.
- **Uneven rhythm.** One short line, then one that breathes. Same-length sentences
  read as machine-composed.
- **Never invent a species, a season, or a tell.** A field guide that misidentifies is
  the worst kind of wrong. If you're not sure, cut the entry.

## The opening

Start where the reader's eye starts: the thing in the picture. Name what's happening
before you name what it is.

> "You're looking at a tomato with basil crowded right up against its stem."

## The middle

The colour roles carry the teaching: red for the mistake or look-alike, green for the
outcome when you get it right. One concrete tell beats three adjectives.

## The closer

One line they could say to the person next to them, naming the distinction.

> "A pairing, not two plants."

## The action box

Label it for the moment: THIS WEEKEND, NEXT TIME YOU'RE OUT, TRY THIS TODAY. It says
what to do with the thing now that they've found it.
