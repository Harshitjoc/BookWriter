# Voice — the weeknight pan

This is the voice for a book the reader cooks from, probably standing up, with flour
somewhere. The `/block` skill reads this file before it writes a word. If you copy this
template, rewrite it in your own voice.

## Who you are writing for

Someone hungry, in a normal kitchen, who wants this to work the first time. They are
not a chef and they do not want a lecture. They will follow the steps literally, so
every number has to be real and every step has to be one they can actually do.

## The rules

- **No em dashes.** Commas and full stops. It is the loudest machine-written tell.
- **Contractions, always.** "don't move it", "it's done", "you'll hear it".
- **Numbers you'd say out loud.** "an inch thick", "3 minutes a side", "twice the
  coffee's weight". Vague amounts ("some salt", "a while") are a broken recipe.
- **The mistake, said plainly.** Name the thing a reader will actually get wrong, and
  put it in red. That sentence is worth more than the recipe.
- **Warm, not cute.** No exclamation marks, no "delicious!" It is a friend showing you
  how, not a menu.
- **Never invent a temperature, a time, or an amount.** If you have not cooked it, do
  not give a number for it.

## The opening

Start with what goes in, never with a paragraph about the dish. In a recipe page the
list of ingredients is the opening; the steps come right after, and nothing sits
between them.

## The middle

Amounts live in the list, not in the steps. Each step is one action, in order, short
enough to read with one eye on the pan. Use the colour roles: terracotta for the heat
and the work, basil for the thing that worked, chilli for the mistake.

## The closer

One line that names the idea under the recipe, so they remember why, not just how.

> "The waiting is part of the cooking."

## The action box

Label it for when they'll cook: TRY THIS TONIGHT, THIS WEEKEND, BEFORE YOU SERVE. It
holds the single hardest step, named, so it is the last thing they read.
